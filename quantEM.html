Quantization of the Electromagnetic Field

In order to quantize the electromagnetic field (in the absence of charge), we can follow a similar process to last semester. The first step is to find independent, canonically conjugated variables \(q\) and \(p\), meaning that they follow these rules: \[ \{q,p\}_{PB}=1 \implies [\hat q, \hat p] = i\hbar \text{ where } q(t) \mapsto \hat q \text{ and } p(t) \mapsto \hat p \] this means that \[ \dot q = \frac{\partial H(q, p)}{\partial p} \text{ and } \dot p = -\frac{\partial H(q,p)}{\partial q} \] Thus we begin by finding a set of independent, canonically conjugated variables.

Using the Coulomb gauge and Gauss units, the classical hamiltonian of the field is: \[ H = \frac{1}{8\pi}\int(|\vec E_\perp|^2 + |\vec B|^2)d^3r \] and via Maxwell's equations, \begin{align} \vec E &= -\frac{1}{c}\partial_t\vec A \\ \vec B &= \vec\nabla\times\vec A \\ \vec\nabla\cdot\vec A &= 0 \end{align} (see how we obtain the vector potential here) where the last line is a result from the Coulomb gauge, which requires \(\vec A\) to be transverse. Clearly, \(\vec E\) and \(\vec B\) ar enot independent, as they are both in terms of \(\vec A\). Thus to find some \(H(q,p)\) where \(q\) and \(p\) are independent, we must parameterize \(\vec A\). Once we do that, we define the Hamiltonian, then find our canonically conjugated variables.

Parameterizing \(\vec A\)

Defining boundary conditions. We do this by first finding the set of constraints on \(\vec A\).
First, obviously, the Coulomb gauge says that \(\vec \nabla\cdot\vec A = 0\).
Second, since \(\vec E\) and \(\vec B\) are observable, \(\vec A \in \mathbb R\).
Third, we notice that our current definitions of \(\vec E\) and \(\vec B\) satisfy the first three of Maxwell's equations: \begin{align} \vec\nabla\cdot\vec E &= 4\pi\rho \\ \vec\nabla\cdot\vec B &= 0 \\ \vec\nabla\times\vec E &= -\frac{1}{c}\partial_t\vec B \end{align} but we still need to satisfy the final one: \begin{align} \vec\nabla\times\vec B &= \frac{4\pi}{c}\vec j + \frac{1}{c}\partial_t\vec E \\ \vec\nabla\times\left(\vec\nabla\times\vec A\right) &= 0 + \frac{1}{c}\partial_t\left(-\frac{1}{c}\partial_t\vec A\right) \\ -\nabla^2\vec A &= -\frac{1}{c}\partial_t^2\vec A \\ \left[\frac{1}{c^2}\partial^2_t - \nabla^2\right] \vec A &= 0 \\ \Box\vec A &= 0 \end{align} where the operator \(\Box\) is called d'Alembertian. \(\Box\vec A = 0\) is our final constraint.

We add another extra feature to our problem space, mostly, it seems, to make some of the integrals easier. \(\vec A\) is defined over all space, however we confine it to a cube with a side length of \(L\) which has periodic boundary conditions. This discretizes momentum space, but later we can take the limit as \(V = L^3 \to \infty\), which yields results that are independent of the discretization chosen. Under this discretization, we have: \[ \vec A(\vec r + \hat x_i L , t) = \vec A(\vec r, t) \text{ for } \vec A(\vec r, t) : [0, L]^3\times \mathbb R \to \mathcal E(3) \] Essentially, we want a \(\vec r\) to be equivalent to \(\vec(r + \hat x_i L)\), where \(\hat x_i = \hat x, \hat y, \hat z\). This means that the behavior of \(\vec A\) at \(\vec r\) is the same as the behavior at \(\vec A\) after we have walked a distance of \(L\) in purely the \(\hat x, \hat y,\) or \(\hat z\) directions, starting at \(\vec r\). Another way to write the vector potential is: \[ \vec A(\vec r, t) = \sum_{\vec k} \vec A_{\vec k}(t) e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \text{ for } \vec k = \frac{2\pi}{L} n_x \hat x + \frac{2\pi}{L} n_y \hat y + \frac{2\pi}{L} n_z \hat z, n_i \in \mathbb Z \] This is apparently a Fourier series. Recall that \(e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r}\) describes a plane wave, or a free particle (sans some prefactor) moving with no potential energy and momentum \(\hbar\vec k\). This means that \(\vec k\) is the wavevector which is equal to \(k\cdot\hat k\), where \(k\) is the wavenumber and \(\hat k\) is the direction the plane wave propogates in. This definition of \(\vec A(\vec r, t)\) matches our boundary conditions: \begin{align} e^{i\vec k\cdot(\vec r + \hat x_i L)} &= e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} \\ \vec k\cdot(\vec r + \hat x_i L) &= \vec k\cdot \vec r + L(\vec k \cdot \hat x_i) = \vec k\cdot\vec r + Lk_i \\ &= \vec k\cdot\vec r + L\frac{2\pi}{L} n_i = \vec k\cdot\vec r + 2\pi n_i \\ e^{i\vec k\cdot(\vec r + \hat x_i L)} &= e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r + 2\pi n_i)} \\ &= e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r}e^{i2\pi n_i} \\ &= e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} \end{align} Notice how \(\vec A(\vec r, t) = \sum_{\vec k} \vec A_{\vec k}(t) e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r}\) separates the time and space dependencies within \(\vec A\) into two separate terms. Also note that \[ \sum_{\vec k} = \sum^\infty_{n_x = -\infty}\sum^\infty_{n_y = -\infty}\sum^\infty_{n_z = -\infty} \] Enforcing boundary conditions. We now have the following conditions to enforce:
  • \(\vec\nabla\cdot\vec A = 0\)
  • \(\vec A\) is real
  • \(\Box \vec A = 0\)
Using our new definition of \(\vec A\), we enforce the first condition: \[ \vec\nabla\cdot\vec A = \vec\nabla\cdot\left(\sum_{\vec k} \vec A_{\vec k}(t) e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r}\right) = \sum_{\vec k} \vec\nabla\cdot\vec A_{\vec k}(t) e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} = 0 \] In general, the divergence of a product between a vector and a function that depends on a position vector, \(\vec\nabla\cdot\vec Af(\vec r)\), can be written as a sum of vector components: \[ \sum_i\partial_i A_if(\vec r) = \sum_i A_i\partial_i f(\vec r) = \vec A\cdot\vec\nabla f(\vec r) \] Thus we have \[ \vec\nabla\cdot\vec A = \sum_{\vec k} \vec A_{\vec k}(t) (\vec\nabla\cdot e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r}) = \sum_{\vec k} \vec A_{\vec k}(t)i\vec ke^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} = 0 \] The term \(e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r}\) is never 0, since it lies on the unit circle in the complex plane. This means that \(\vec A_{\vec k}(t)\cdot\vec k=0, \forall \vec k\), or in other words, that \(\vec A_{\vec k}(t)\) must be perpendicular to \(\vec k\). Since we are in a three-dimensional space, we can find two unit vectors that form an orthonormal basis with \(\hat k\). Call these \(\hat\epsilon_{k,1}\) and \(\hat\epsilon_{k,2}\).

We say that \(\hat\epsilon_{k,i}\cdot\hat\epsilon_{k,j}^* = \delta_{ij}\) and that \(\hat\epsilon_{-\vec k, i} \equiv \hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i}^*\) for \(i, j\in\{1,2\}\) and, of course, that \(\hat\epsilon_{k,i}\cdot\vec k = 0\). Since \(\vec A_{\vec k}(t)\) is perpendicular to \(\hat k\), it must be in the plane of \(\hat\epsilon_{k,1}\) and \(\hat\epsilon_{k,2}\), we can write \(\vec A_{\vec k}(t)\) as a linear combination of the two: \[ \vec A_{\vec k}(t) = \sum_{i=1}^2 A_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \] for time-dependent coefficient \(A_{\vec k, i}(t)\). Note that as a result, if we have well defined \(\vec k\) and \(i\), we have defined an specific mode with wavevector \(\vec k\) and polarization \(i\). The vector field (or one of its components) propagates along \(\vec k\), and \(i\) indicates which transverse unit vector \(\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i}\) the vector potential (or one of its components) varies along.
It is possible to have a single fixed \(\vec k\) and a combination of \(\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i}\)'s, in which you would still have transverse polarization, just at an angle.

but we can treat such cases as two components polarized along different \(\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i}\)'s.

Anyway, if we define \(\vec A_{\vec k}(t)\) in this way, then \(\vec A(\vec r, t) = \sum_{\vec k} \vec A_{\vec k}(t) e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r}\) satisfies the first constraint that \(\vec\nabla\cdot\vec A = 0\). In other words, we have found that this definition of \(\vec A(\vec r, t)\): \[ \vec A(\vec r, t) = \sum_{\vec k, i} A_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \tag{1} \] meets the first requirement.
We now enforce the next requirement that \(\vec A(\vec r, t)\) is real, or that \[ \vec A(\vec r, t) = \vec A(\vec r, t)^* \] Using (1), \[ \vec A(\vec r, t)^* = \sum_{\vec k, i} A^*_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \] Because \(\vec k\) can range from negative to positive, we can simply swap all \(\vec k\) with \(-\vec k\): \[ \vec A(\vec r, t)^* = \sum_{-\vec k, i} A^*_{-\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon^*_{-\vec k, i} e^{(-i)-\vec k \cdot\vec r} \] and we said that \(\hat\epsilon_{-\vec k, i} \equiv \hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i}^*\), so \(\hat\epsilon^*_{-\vec k, i} = \hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i}\): \[ \vec A(\vec r, t)^* = \sum_{-\vec k, i} A^*_{-\vec k, i}(t) \hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \] We then do the following: \begin{align} \vec A(\vec r, t) &= \vec A(\vec r, t)^* \\ \vec A(\vec r, t) - \vec A(\vec r, t)^* &= 0 \\ \left(\sum_{\vec k, i} A_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \right) - \left(\sum_{-\vec k, i} A^*_{-\vec k, i}(t) \hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r}\right) &= 0 \\ \sum_{\vec k, i} \left( A_{\vec k, i}(t) - A^*_{-\vec k, i}(t)\right)\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} &= 0 \\ \implies A_{\vec k, i}(t) - A^*_{-\vec k, i}(t) &= 0, \forall \vec k, i \end{align} Essentially we have found that in order for \(\vec A(\vec r, t)\) to be real, the coefficients that describe \(\vec A(\vec r, t)\) in the plane perpendicular to \(\vec k\) must follow: \[ A_{\vec k, i}(t) = A^*_{-\vec k, i}(t) \tag{2} \] We now enforce the final constraint that \(\Box\vec A = 0\). \begin{align} \Box\vec A(\vec r, t) &= \Box \sum_{\vec k, i} A_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \text{ using (1)} \\ &= \left[\frac{1}{c^2}\partial^2_t - \nabla^2\right]\sum_{\vec k, i} A_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i}\left(\frac{1}{c^2}\ddot A_{\vec k, i}(t) + k^2 A_{\vec k, i}(t)\right) \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \end{align} since \(-\nabla^2 A_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r}\) is equal to \(A_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \left(-\nabla^2e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r}\right) = A_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} k^2 e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r}\). Setting this equal to 0 indicates that \(\frac{1}{c^2}\ddot A_{\vec k, i}(t) + k^2 A_{\vec k, i}(t)=0\), which results in a second-order homogenous equation: \[ \ddot A_{\vec k, i}(t) + k^2c^2A_{\vec k, i}(t) = 0 \] Remember that \(\vec k\) is our momentum vector (and what \(\vec k\)'s are allowed is restricted by our discretization). We are describing the motion of particles in the electromagnetic field, so they all move at the speed of light, or at \(c\). This has units of \([L]/[T]\), and our wavenumber \(k\) has units of \(1/[L]\), so \([kc] = 1/[T]\), or units of frequency. As a result, we can define \[\omega = ck\] which means that our second-order equation is: \[ \ddot A_{\vec k, i}(t) + \omega^2 A_{\vec k, i}(t) = 0 \] which has closed form solution: \begin{align} A_{\vec k, i}(t) &= C^+_{\vec k, i}(t=0) e^{i\omega t} + C^-_{\vec k, i}(t=0) e^{-i\omega t} \\ &= C^+_{\vec k, i}(t) + C^-_{\vec k, i}(t) \tag{3} \end{align} for \(C^\pm_{\vec k, i}(t=0) \in \mathbb C\). You might be more used to seeing it in this form: \[ A_{\vec k, i}(t) = C_1\cos(\omega t) + C_2\sin(\omega t) \] but we can show that they're the same as follows: \begin{align} A_{\vec k, i}(t) &= C_1\cos(\omega t) + C_2\sin(\omega t) \\ &= C_1\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{i\omega t} + e^{-i\omega t}\right) + C_2\frac{1}{2i}\left(e^{i\omega t}-e^{-i\omega t}\right) \\ &= \left(\frac{C_1}{2} + \frac{C_2}{2i}\right)e^{i\omega t} + \left(\frac{C_1}{2} - \frac{C_2}{2i}\right)e^{-i\omega t} \end{align} where \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) are determined by initial conditions, hence we can write \(\left(\frac{C_1}{2} + \frac{C_2}{2i}\right)\) as \(C^+(t=0)\) and \(\left(\frac{C_1}{2} - \frac{C_2}{2i}\right)\) as \(C^-(t=0)\).

We now use (2): \begin{align} A_{\vec k, i}(t) &= A^*_{-\vec k, i}(t) \\ C^+_{\vec k, i}(0)e^{i\omega t} + C^-_{\vec k, i}(0)e^{-i\omega t} &= C^{+*}_{-\vec k, i}(0)e^{-i\omega t} + C^{-*}_{-\vec k, i}(0)e^{i\omega t} \\ \left[C^+_{\vec k, i}(0) - C^{-*}_{-\vec k, i}(0)\right] e^{i\omega t} &= \left[C^{+*}_{-\vec k, i}(0) - C^-_{\vec k, i}(0)\right]e^{-i\omega t} \\ \left[C^+_{\vec k, i}(0) - C^{-*}_{-\vec k, i}(0)\right] e^{i\omega t} &- \left[C^{+*}_{-\vec k, i}(0) - C^-_{\vec k, i}(0)\right]e^{-i\omega t} = 0, \forall t \\ C^+_{\vec k, i}(0) = C^{-*}_{-\vec k, i}(0) &\text{ and } C^{+*}_{-\vec k, i}(0) = C^-_{\vec k, i}(0) \\ C^\pm_{\vec k, i}(0) &= C^{\mp*}_{-\vec k, i}(0) \\ C^\pm_{\vec k, i}(t) &= C^{\mp*}_{-\vec k, i}(t) \end{align} We now use (3) in (1): \begin{align} \vec A(\vec r, t) &= \sum_{\vec k, i} A_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \left(C^+_{\vec k, i}(t) + C^-_{\vec k, i}(t)\right) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} C^+_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} + \sum_{\vec k, i} C^-_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \\ \end{align} Since every \(\vec k\) has a corresponding \(-\vec k\) and we're ranging over all \(\vec k\), we can re-write the first term using \(-\vec k\) and still get the same result: \begin{align} \vec A(\vec r, t) &= \sum_{\vec k, i} C^+_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} + \sum_{\vec k, i} C^-_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} C^+_{-\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{-\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k \cdot\vec r} + \sum_{\vec k, i} C^-_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} C^{-*}_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k \cdot\vec r} + \sum_{\vec k, i} C^-_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} C^{-*}_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k \cdot\vec r} + C^-_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \tag{4} \end{align} using \(\hat\epsilon_{-\vec k, i} \equiv \hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i}^*\). We now have an expression for \(\vec A(\vec r, t)\) that satisfies all constraints and depends only on parameter \(C^{-}_{\vec k, i}(0)\).

Finding the Hamiltonian

We can now define our Hamiltonian. Classically, the Hamiltonian of the electromagnetic field is: \[ H = \frac{1}{8\pi}\int d^3 r(E_\perp^2 + B^2) \] Now that we have rigorously defined \(\vec A(\vec r, t)\), we can come up with an exact expression for the Hamiltonian. Recall that \(\vec E = -\frac{1}{c}\partial_t\vec A\) and \(\vec B = \vec\nabla\times\vec A \). We use (1) for now just for convenience, and fill in with (3) later. \begin{align} \vec E_\perp &= -\frac{1}{c}\partial_t \left(\sum_{\vec k, i} A_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r}\right) \\ &= -\frac{1}{c} \sum_{\vec k, i} \dot A_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \\ \end{align} Since \(\vec E_\perp\) and \(\vec B\) are real, \(\vec E_\perp\cdot\vec E_\perp = \vec E_\perp\cdot \vec E_\perp^*\): \begin{align} \vec E_\perp &= -\frac{1}{c} \sum_{\vec k, i} \dot A_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \\ \vec E^2_\perp &= \frac{1}{c^2}\sum_{\vec k, i}\sum_{\vec k', j} \dot A_{\vec k, i}(t) \dot A^*_{\vec k', j}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i}\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k', j} e^{i(\vec k -\vec k')\cdot\vec r} \\ \int d^3 r \vec E^2_\perp &= \frac{1}{c^2}\sum_{\vec k, i}\sum_{\vec k', j} \dot A_{\vec k, i}(t) \dot A^*_{\vec k', j}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i}\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k', j} \int d^3 r e^{i(\vec k -\vec k')\cdot\vec r} \tag{5} \end{align} We can see that \[ \int_V d^3r\, e^{i(\vec k-\vec k')\cdot \vec r} = V\,\delta_{\vec k\vec k'} \] by doing: \begin{align} \vec r &= x\hat x+y\hat y+z\hat z \\ (\vec k-\vec k')\cdot \vec r &= (k_x-k_x')x+(k_y-k_y')y+(k_z-k_z')z \\ \int_V d^3r\, e^{i(\vec k-\vec k')\cdot \vec r} &= \int_0^L dx\int_0^L dy\int_0^L dz\, e^{i[(k_x-k_x')x+(k_y-k_y')y+(k_z-k_z')z]} \\ &= \left(\int_0^L dx\, e^{i(k_x-k_x')x}\right) \left(\int_0^L dy\, e^{i(k_y-k_y')y}\right) \left(\int_0^L dz\, e^{i(k_z-k_z')z}\right) \\ &= \int_0^L dx\, e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_x-n_x')x} \int_0^L dy\, e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_y-n_y')y} \int_0^L dz\, e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_z-n_z')z} \end{align} If \(n_i = n_i'\), then each exponential becomes \(e^0=1\). This means that each term yields \(L\), so altogether, \(\int_V d^3r\, e^{i(\vec k-\vec k')\cdot \vec r} = L^3\).

If \(n_i \neq n_i'\), then we must do: \begin{align} \int_V d^3r\, e^{i(\vec k-\vec k')\cdot \vec r} &= \int_0^L dx\, e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_x-n_x')x} \int_0^L dy\, e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_y-n_y')y} \int_0^L dz\, e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_z-n_z')z} \\ &= \left[ \frac{e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_x-n_x')x}}{i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_x-n_x')} \right]_0^L \left[ \frac{e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_y-n_y')y}}{i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_y-n_y')} \right]_0^L \left[ \frac{e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_z-n_z')z}}{i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_z-n_z')} \right]_0^L \\ &= \frac{ e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_x-n_x')L}-1 }{ i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_x-n_x') } \frac{ e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_y-n_y')L}-1 }{ i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_y-n_y') } \frac{ e^{i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_z-n_z')L}-1 }{ i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_z-n_z') } \\ &= \frac{ 1-1 }{ i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_x-n_x') } \frac{ 1-1 }{ i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_y-n_y') } \frac{ 1-1 }{ i\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_z-n_z') } \\ &= 0 \end{align} So, we either get \(L^3=V\) or we get 0. The only time \(n_i = n_i', \forall i\) is when \(\vec k = \vec k'\). This means \(\int_V d^3r\, e^{i(\vec k-\vec k')\cdot \vec r} = V\delta_{\vec k\vec k'}\). Using this in (5) gives us: \begin{align} \int d^3 r \vec E^2_\perp &= \frac{1}{c^2}\sum_{\vec k, i}\sum_{\vec k', j} \dot A_{\vec k, i}(t) \dot A^*_{\vec k', j}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i}\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k', j} e^{i(\vec k -\vec k')\cdot\vec r} \\ &= \frac{V}{c^2}\sum_{\vec k, i}\sum_j \dot A_{\vec k, i}(t) \dot A^*_{\vec k, j}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i}\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, j} \\ &= \frac{V}{c^2}\sum_{\vec k, i}\sum_j \dot A_{\vec k, i}(t) \dot A^*_{\vec k, j}(t) \hat \delta_{ij} \\ &= \frac{V}{c^2}\sum_{\vec k, i} \dot A_{\vec k, i}(t) \dot A^*_{\vec k, i}(t) \tag{6} \end{align} Now for \(\vec B\): \begin{align} \vec B &= \vec\nabla\times\vec A = \vec\nabla\times\left(\sum_{\vec k, i} A_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r}\right) \\ \end{align} Using the property that: \[ \vec\nabla\times\vec af(\vec r) = \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk}\partial_j\vec a_kf(\vec r) = \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk}(\partial_j f(\vec r)) a_k = (\vec\nabla f(\vec r))\times \vec a \] we can write \begin{align} \vec B &= \vec\nabla\times\left(\sum_{\vec k, i} A_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r}\right) \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} A_{\vec k, i}\left(\vec\nabla e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r}\right)\times\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} A_{\vec k, i}i\vec ke^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r}\times\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \\ \vec B^2 = \vec B\cdot\vec B^* &= \sum_{\vec k, i} A_{\vec k, i}i\vec ke^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r}\times\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \sum_{\vec k', j} A^*_{\vec k', j}(-i)\vec k'e^{-i\vec k' \cdot\vec r}\times\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k', j} \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i, \vec k', j} A_{\vec k, i}A^*_{\vec k', j}(\vec k\times\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i})(\vec k'\times\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k', j})e^{i(\vec k - \vec k')\cdot\vec r} \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i, \vec k', j} A_{\vec k, i}A^*_{\vec k', j}(\vec k\times\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i})(\vec k'\times\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k', j})V\delta_{\vec k\vec k'} \\ \int d^3r\vec B^2 &= V\sum_{\vec k, i, j} A_{\vec k, i}A^*_{\vec k, j}(\vec k\times\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i})(\vec k\times\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, j}) \\ \text{Using } \vec k &= k\hat k \\ &= V\sum_{\vec k, i, j} k^2A_{\vec k, i}A^*_{\vec k, j}(\vec k\times\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i})(\vec k\times\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, j}) \\ \end{align} We use the identities \(\vec a \cdot (\vec b\times \vec c) = \vec b\cdot(\vec c\times\vec a)\) and \(\vec a \times(\vec b\times \vec c) = \vec b (\vec a \cdot\vec c)\): \begin{align} (\hat k\times\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i})(\hat k\times\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, j}) &= \hat k \cdot (\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, j}^*\times(\hat k \times \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i})) \\ &= \hat k \cdot \hat k(\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, j}\cdot\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i}) - \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i}(\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, j}\cdot\hat k) \\ &= \hat k\cdot \hat k\delta_{ij} - 0 \\ &= \delta_{ij} \\ \int d^3r\vec B^2 &= V\sum_{\vec k, i, j} k^2A_{\vec k, i}A^*_{\vec k, j}(\vec k\times\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i})(\vec k\times\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, j}) \\ &= V\sum_{\vec k, i, j} k^2A_{\vec k, i}A^*_{\vec k, j}\delta_{ij} \\ &= V\sum_{\vec k, i} k^2A_{\vec k, i}A^*_{\vec k, i} \\ &= \frac{V}{c^2}\sum_{\vec k, i} \omega^2A_{\vec k, i}A^*_{\vec k, i} \tag{7} \end{align} If we plug (6) and (7) into the classical Hamiltonian, we get \begin{align} H &= \frac{1}{8\pi}\int d^3 r(E_\perp^2 + B^2) = \frac{1}{8\pi}\left(\frac{V}{c^2}\sum_{\vec k, i} \dot A_{\vec k, i}(t) \dot A^*_{\vec k, i}(t) + \frac{V}{c^2}\sum_{\vec k, i} \omega^2A_{\vec k, i}A^*_{\vec k, i} \right) \\ &= \frac{V}{8\pi c^2}\sum_{\vec k, i} \dot A_{\vec k, i}(t) \dot A^*_{\vec k, i}(t) + \omega^2A_{\vec k, i}A^*_{\vec k, i} \end{align} and now, if we use (3): \begin{align} A_{\vec k, i}(t) &= C^+_{\vec k, i}(t=0) e^{i\omega t} + C^-_{\vec k, i}(t=0) e^{-i\omega t} \\ &= C^+_{\vec k, i}(t) + C^-_{\vec k, i}(t) \\ \dot A_{\vec k, i}(t) &= i\omega(C^+_{\vec k, i}(t) - C^-_{\vec k, i}(t)) \\ \dot A_{\vec k, i}(t)\dot A^*_{\vec k, i}(t) &= \omega^2\left[C^-_{\vec k, i}(t)C^{-*}_{\vec k, i}(t) - C^-_{\vec k, i}(t)C^{+*}_{\vec k, i}(t) - C^+_{\vec k, i}(t)C^{-*}_{\vec k, i}(t) + C^+_{\vec k, i}(t)C^{+*}_{\vec k, i}(t)\right] \\ A_{\vec k, i}(t) A^*_{\vec k, i}(t) &= \omega^2\left[C^-_{\vec k, i}(t)C^{-*}_{\vec k, i}(t) + C^-_{\vec k, i}(t)C^{+*}_{\vec k, i}(t) + C^+_{\vec k, i}(t)C^{-*}_{\vec k, i}(t) + C^+_{\vec k, i}(t)C^{+*}_{\vec k, i}(t)\right] \\ H &= \frac{V}{8\pi c^2}\sum_{\vec k, i} 2\omega^2\left[C^-_{\vec k, i}(t)C^{-*}_{\vec k, i}(t) + C^+_{\vec k, i}(t)C^{+*}_{\vec k, i}(t)\right] \\ &= \frac{V}{4\pi c^2}\left[\sum_{\vec k, i} \omega^2C^-_{\vec k, i}(t)C^{-*}_{\vec k, i}(t) + \sum_{\vec k, i} \omega^2 C^+_{-\vec k, i}(t)C^{+*}_{-\vec k, i}(t)\right] \\ &= \frac{V}{4\pi c^2}\left[\sum_{\vec k, i} \omega^2C^-_{\vec k, i}(t)C^{-*}_{\vec k, i}(t) + \sum_{\vec k, i} \omega^2 C^{-*}_{\vec k, i}(t)C^{-}_{\vec k, i}(t)\right] \\ &= \frac{V}{2\pi c^2}\sum_{\vec k, i} \omega^2C^-_{\vec k, i}(t)C^{-*}_{\vec k, i}(t) \end{align} Note that although it is not really clearly stated, \(\omega\) has a dependence on \(\vec k\) and thus cannot be pulled out of the summation.

Finding canonically conjugated variables

Now that we have our Hamiltonian rigorously defined, we can finally identify proper variables that are real, independent, and canonically conjugated. We thus define time-dependent coefficient \(\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t)\) where: \[ \alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) \equiv \sqrt{\frac{V\omega^2}{2\pi c^2}}C^-_{\vec k, i}(t) \in \mathbb C \tag{8} \] so that we can define the classical Hamiltonian as: \[ H = \sum_{\vec k, i}\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t)\alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t) \] From here, we can define: \[ q_{\vec k, i}(t) = \frac{1}{\omega\sqrt 2}(\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) + \alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t)) \text{ and } p_{\vec k, i}(t) = \frac{1}{i\sqrt 2}(\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) - \alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t)) \] which are real operators (and so can be associated with self-adjoint operators). We can write \(\alpha_{\vec k, i}\) in terms of \(q_{\vec k, i}\) and \(p_{\vec k, i}\) if we want: \[ \alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}(\omega q_{\vec k, i}(t) + ip_{\vec k, i}(t)) \text{ and } \alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}(\omega q_{\vec k, i}(t) - ip_{\vec k, i}(t)) \] which makes it clear that \(q_{\vec k, i}\) is the real part of \(\alpha_{\vec k, i}\) and \(p_{\vec k, i}\) is the imaginary part of \(\alpha_{\vec k, i}\). We can also write the Hamiltonian in terms of these variables: \[ H = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\vec k, i}(\omega^2 q^2_{\vec k, i}(t) + p^2_{\vec k, i}(t)) \tag{9} \] Let's check to see if \(q_{\vec k, i}\) and \(p_{\vec k, i}\) are actually canonically conjugated, or that \(\dot q_{\vec k, i}(t) = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{\vec k, i}(t)}\) and \(\dot p_{\vec k, i}(t) = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_{\vec k, i}(t)}\). From (9) we can see that \(\partial_{p_{\vec k, i}} H \) (so, for a specific \(\vec k, i\)) is \(\frac{1}{2}2p_{\vec k, i} = p_{\vec k, i}\), and that \(\partial_{q_{\vec k, i}}H = \omega^2q_{\vec k, i}\). Calculating the time-derivatives of \(q_{\vec k, i}(t)\) and \(p_{\vec k, i}(t)\), we get: \begin{align} \dot q_{\vec k, i}(t) &= \partial_t\left(\frac{1}{\omega\sqrt 2}(\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) + \alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t))\right) \\ &= \frac{1}{\omega\sqrt 2}(\dot\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) + \dot\alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t)) \\ &\text{Using (3) and (8):} \\ &= \frac{1}{\omega\sqrt 2}\left\{\sqrt{\frac{V\omega^2}{2\pi c^2}}C^-_{\vec k, i}(0)\partial_t e^{-i\omega t} + \sqrt{\frac{V\omega^2}{2\pi c^2}}C^{*-}_{\vec k, i}(0)\partial_t e^{i\omega t}\right\} \\ &= \frac{1}{\omega\sqrt 2}(-i\omega)\left(\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) - \alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t)\right) = \frac{1}{i\sqrt 2}\left(\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) - \alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t)\right) \\ &= p_{\vec k, i}(t) \checkmark \\ \dot p_{\vec k, i}(t) &= \partial_t \left(\frac{1}{i\sqrt 2}\left(\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) - \alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t)\right)\right) \\ &= \frac{1}{i\sqrt 2}\left(\dot\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) - \dot\alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t)\right) \\ &= \frac{1}{i\sqrt 2}(-i\omega)\left(\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) - \alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t)\right) \\ &= -\frac{\omega}{\sqrt 2}\left(\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) - \alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t)\right) = -\omega^2\frac{1}{\omega\sqrt 2}(\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) + \alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t)) \\ &= -\omega^2q_{\vec k, i}(t) \checkmark \end{align} Thus, \(\dot q_{\vec k, i}(t) = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{\vec k, i}(t)}\) and \(\dot p_{\vec k, i}(t) = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_{\vec k, i}(t)}\) holds true and \(q_{\vec k, i}(t)\) and \(p_{\vec k, i}(t)\) are the independent, canonically conjugated variables that we've been looking for.

Quantizing the EM Field

Putting this all together, we now have: \begin{align} q_{\vec k, i}(t) \mapsto \hat q_{\vec k, i}(t) &\text{ and } p_{\vec k, i}(t) \mapsto \hat p_{\vec k, i}(t) \\ q^\dagger_{\vec k, i}(t) \mapsto \hat q^\dagger_{\vec k, i}(t) &\text{ and } p^\dagger_{\vec k, i}(t) \mapsto \hat p^\dagger_{\vec k, i}(t) \\ [\hat q_{\vec k, i}(t), \hat q_{\vec k, j}(t)] &= [\hat p_{\vec k, i}(t), \hat p_{\vec k, j}(t)] = 0 \\ [\hat q_{\vec k, i}(t), \hat p_{\vec k', j}(t)] &= i\hbar\delta_{\vec k\vec k'}\delta_{ij} \\ H \mapsto \hat H &= \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\vec k, i}(\omega^2 \hat q^2_{\vec k, i}(t) + \hat p^2_{\vec k, i}(t)) \end{align} Like in the case of the harmonic oscillator, we can introduce \(\hat Q_{\vec k, i}(t)\) and \(\hat P_{\vec k, i}(t)\) which are dimensionless. \[ \hat Q_{\vec k, i}(t) = \sqrt{\frac{\omega}{\hbar}}\hat q_{\vec k, i}(t) \text{ and } \hat P_{\vec k, i}(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\hbar\omega}}\hat p_{\vec k, i}(t) \] which means that \([\hat Q_{\vec k, i}(t), \hat P_{\vec k, i}(t)] = i\delta_{\vec k, \vec k'}\delta_{ij}\) and \(\hat H = \sum_{\vec k, i}\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}(\hat Q^2_{\vec k, i}(t) + \hat P^2_{\vec k, i}(t))\). We can, in turn, also define annihilation and creation operators: \[ \hat a_{\vec k, i}(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}(\hat Q_{\vec k, i}(t) + i\hat P_{\vec k, i}(t)) \text{ and } \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}(\hat Q_{\vec k, i}(t) - i\hat P_{\vec k, i}(t)) \] from which we define \[ \hat H = \sum_{\vec k, i} \hbar\omega(\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}(t)\hat a_{\vec k, i}(t) + \frac{1}{2}) \] We can also define the number operator: \begin{align} \hat n_{\vec k, i}(t) &= \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}(t)\hat a_{\vec k, i}(t) \\ \hat n_{\vec k, i}(t)|n\rangle &= n\ket n, n\in \{0, 1, 2, \cdots\} \\ \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}(t)\ket n = \sqrt{n+1}\ket {n+1}&, \hat a_{\vec k, i}(t)\ket n = \sqrt n\ket{n-1} \end{align} from which we can see that the creation and annihilation opperators for a specific mode add and remove photons in that mode and using the number operator, the total Hamiltonian is: \[ \hat H = \sum_{\vec k, i} \hbar\omega(\hat n_{\vec k, i}(t) + \frac{1}{2}) \] We can thus think of the quantized electromagnetic field as an infinite number of independent harmonic oscillators, each one corresponding to a specific mode \({\vec k, i}\) (recall that \(\vec k\) describes the direction of prpoagation and \(i\) labels the polarization), or that \(\hat H = \sum_{\vec k, i} \hat H_{\vec k, i} \otimes \hat 1 \otimes \hat 1 \cdots\) where the \(\hat 1\)'s correspond to all other modes.
Each individual field mode has the same Hilbert space as the 1-dimensional harmonic oscillator: \[ \mathcal E_{\vec k, i} = \text{span}\{\ket 0, \ket 1, \ket 2, \cdots\} = \{\sum_{n=0}^\infty \ket n c_n, \sum_{n=0}^\infty |c_n|^2 \lt \infty\} \] and the basis for the total Hilbert space is the tensor product of basis states for each of these inividiual modes: \[ \ket n = |n_{\vec k_1, 1}\rangle \otimes |n_{\vec k_1, 2}\rangle \otimes |n_{\vec k_2, 1}\rangle \otimes |n_{\vec k_2, 2}\rangle \otimes \cdots \] Because \(q_{\vec k, i}(t)\) and \(p_{\vec k, i}(t)\) are real, the state \(\ket{\psi_{\vec k, i}}\) for a single mode is square-integrable and the full Hilbert space is: \[ V = \bigotimes_{\vec k, i}L^2(\mathbb R) \] with one \(L^2(\mathbb R)\) for each mode.

We often write the total state of the field over all modes as the tensor product of the number of photons in each mode. For example: \[ \ket\psi = \ket 1 \otimes \ket 0 \otimes \ket 1 \otimes \ket 7 \otimes \cdots = \ket{1,0,1,7,\cdots} \] if there is 1 photon in the first mode, none in the second, 1 in the third, and 7 in the fourth, and so on. We call the state where there are no photons in any mode the "vacuum state", which is often written as \(\ket {0, 0, 0,\cdots} = \ket 0\) or as \(\ket -\).

We can also write any state as a series of annihilation and creation operators acting on the vacuum state. For example, if we have a state with two photons in mode \(\vec k, i\) and five photons in mode \(\vec k', j\), we would write: \[ |\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2!}}(\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i})^2 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{5!}}(\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k', j})^5 \ket - \]

Defining the Field Operators

Since we use vectors \(\ket n\) (over all modes) to describe the state of the field, it is more convenient to define our field operators (and everything else) in terms of \(\hat a_{\vec k, i}(t)\) and \(\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}(t)\) instead of in terms of \(\hat q_{\vec k, i}(t)\) and \(\hat p_{\vec k, i}(t)\).

Quantizing \(\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t)\) Notice that \(q_{\vec k, i}(t)\) and \(p_{\vec k, i}(t)\) are in terms of \(\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t)\), so in quantizing those, we imply that \(\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t)\) are quantized as well: \begin{align} q_{\vec k, i}(t) \mapsto \hat q_{\vec k, i}(t) &= \frac{1}{\omega\sqrt 2}(\hat\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) + \hat\alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t)) \tag{10a} \\ p_{\vec k, i}(t) \mapsto p_{\vec k, i}(t) &= \frac{1}{i\sqrt 2}(\hat\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) - \hat\alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t)) \tag{10b} \end{align} and we can write \(\hat \alpha_{\vec k, i}\) in terms of the annihilation and creation operators. To do this, we first write \(\hat a_{\vec k, i}(t)\) in terms of \(\hat q_{\vec k, i}\) and \(\hat p_{\vec k, i}\) using \(\hat Q_{\vec k, i}(t) = \sqrt{\frac{\omega}{\hbar}}\hat q_{\vec k, i}(t)\) and \(\hat P_{\vec k, i}(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\hbar\omega}}\hat p_{\vec k, i}(t)\): \begin{align} \hat a_{\vec k, i}(t) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}(\hat Q_{\vec k, i}(t) + i\hat P_{\vec k, i}(t)) \\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}(\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{\hbar}}\hat q_{\vec k, i}(t) + i\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hbar\omega}}\hat p_{\vec k, i}(t)) \\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\hbar\omega}} (\omega \hat q_{\vec k, i}(t) + i\hat p_{\vec k, i}(t)) \\ \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}(t) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\hbar\omega}} (\omega \hat q_{\vec k, i}(t) - i\hat p_{\vec k, i}(t)) \\ \end{align} and then plug in (10a) and (10b): \begin{align} \hat a_{\vec k, i}(t) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\hbar\omega}} (\omega \frac{1}{\omega\sqrt 2}(\hat\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) + \hat\alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t)) + i\frac{1}{i\sqrt 2}(\hat\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) - \hat\alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t))) \\ &= \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\hbar\omega}}((\hat\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) + \hat\alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t)) + (\hat\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) - \hat\alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t))) \\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{\hbar\omega}}\hat\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) \\ \hat\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) &= \sqrt{\hbar\omega} \hat a_{\vec k, i}(t) \\ \hat\alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t) &= \sqrt{\hbar\omega}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}(t) \\ \end{align} Quantizing \(A_{\vec k, i}(t)\). Now that we have the quanitzed form of \(\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t)\) in terms of the annihilation and creation operators, we can also do the same for \(A_{\vec k, i}(t)\). Starting with classical (3) and (8), we can find \(A_{\vec k, i}(t)\) in terms of \(\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t)\): \begin{align} \alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) &= \sqrt{\frac{V\omega^2}{2\pi c^2}}C^-_{\vec k, i}(t) \qquad \text{ From (8)} \\ C^-_{\vec k, i}(t) &= \sqrt{\frac{2\pi c^2}{V\omega^2}}\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) \\ A_{\vec k, i}(t) &= C^+_{\vec k, i}(t) + C^-_{\vec k, i}(t) \qquad \text{ From (3)} \\ \text{Using } C^\pm_{\vec k, i}(t) &= C^{\mp*}_{-\vec k, i}(t) \\ &= C^{*-}_{-\vec k, i}(t) + C^-_{\vec k, i}(t) \\ &= \left(\sqrt{\frac{2\pi c^2}{V\omega^2}}\alpha_{-\vec k, i}(t)\right)^* + \sqrt{\frac{2\pi c^2}{V\omega^2}}\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) \\ &= \sqrt\frac{2\pi c^2}{V\omega^2}(\alpha^*_{-\vec k, i}(t) + \alpha_{\vec k, i}(t)) \end{align} Thus because \begin{align} \alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) \mapsto \hat\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t) &= \sqrt{\hbar\omega} \hat a_{\vec k, i}(t) \\ \alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t) \mapsto \hat\alpha^*_{\vec k, i}(t) &= \sqrt{\hbar\omega}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}(t) \\ \end{align} we can also say that \begin{align} A_{\vec k, i}(t) \mapsto \hat A_{\vec k, i}(t) &= \sqrt\frac{2\pi c^2}{V\omega^2}(\hat\alpha^*_{-\vec k, i}(t) + \hat\alpha_{\vec k, i}(t)) \\ &= \sqrt\frac{2\pi c^2}{V\omega^2} (\sqrt{\hbar\omega}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}(t) + \sqrt{\hbar\omega}\hat a_{-\vec k, i}(t)) \\ &= \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega}(\hat a^\dagger_{-\vec k, i}(t) + \hat a_{\vec k, i}(t)) \\ &= \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega}(\hat a^\dagger_{-\vec k, i}(t) + \hat a_{\vec k, i}(t)) \end{align} Remember that \(A_{\vec k, i}(t)\) was our time-dependent expansion coefficient for the vector potential. Using (1), we can write the vector potential in terms of the annihilation and creation operators: \begin{align} \vec A(\vec r, t) \mapsto \hat {\vec A}(\vec r, t) &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \hat A_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} (\hat a^\dagger_{-\vec k, i}(t) + \hat a_{\vec k, i}(t)) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} \hat a^\dagger_{-\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} + \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} \hat a_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{-\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k \cdot\vec r} + \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} \hat a_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \\ \text{Using } \hat\epsilon_{-\vec k, i} &\equiv \hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i}^* \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k \cdot\vec r} + \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} \hat a_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} \left[ \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i} \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k \cdot\vec r} + \hat a_{\vec k, i} \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r}\right] \tag{11} \end{align} and we can also write the electric and magnetic fields in terms of the annihilation and creation operators: \begin{align} \vec E_\perp &= -\frac{1}{c} \left(\sum_{\vec k, i} \dot A_{\vec k, i}(t) \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r}\right) \qquad\text{ See ~(5)} \\ &= -\frac{1}{c} \sum_{\vec k, i} (i\omega C^+_{\vec k, i} - \sqrt{\hbar\omega} \hat a^\dagger_{-\vec k, i}(t))\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} i\sqrt{\frac{h\omega}{V}}( \hat a_{\vec k, i}(t) - \hat a^\dagger_{-\vec k, i}(t))\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \\ &\text{(Some summation reshuffling...)} \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} i\sqrt{\frac{h\omega}{V}}\left(\hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k \cdot\vec r}\right) \tag{12} \\ \hat {\vec B} &= \sum_{\vec k, i} i\sqrt{\frac{h\omega}{V}} \left(\hat a_{\vec k, i}(\hat k \times\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i}) e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r}- \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}(\hat k \times\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i}) e^{-i\vec k \cdot\vec r}\right) \tag{13} \end{align}

The Operators for Linear and Angular Momenta

Finding linear momentum. Recall the unitary translation operator from last semester: \[ \hat T_{\Delta\vec r} = e^{-i\Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}/\hbar} \] If we find the expectation value of some (position-dependent) operator \(\hat{\vec A}(\vec r)\) at position \(\vec r\) of a state that has already been translated a distance \(\Delta \vec r\), we should get the exact same result as when we take the expectation value at position \(\vec r - \Delta\vec r\) before the state was translated. In other words, if \(\hat T_{\Delta\vec r}|\psi\rangle = |\psi'\rangle\): \[ \langle\hat{\vec A}(\vec r)\rangle_{\psi'} = \langle \hat{\vec A}(\vec r-\Delta\vec r)\rangle_\psi \] We can thus also think of applying \(\hat{\vec A}(\vec r-\Delta\vec r)\) to state \(\ket\psi\) as applying \(\hat T^\dagger_{\Delta\vec r}\hat{\vec A}(\vec r)\hat T_{\Delta\vec r}\) to \(\ket\psi\); essentially we translate the state by \(\Delta\vec r\), apply \(\hat{\vec A}(\vec r)\) instead, then translate the state back to its original position. This means we align the portion of \(\ket\psi\) that was at \(\vec r - \Delta\vec r\) to now be at \(\vec r\), measuring at \(\vec r\), and then aligning the state to where it used to be. We thus define: \[ \hat{\vec A}(\vec r-\Delta\vec r) = \hat T^\dagger_{\Delta\vec r}\hat{\vec A}(\vec r)\hat T_{\Delta\vec r} \] Let's say that this operator is our quantized vector potential operator. We can use infinitesimal expansion to write: \begin{align} \hat{\vec A}(\vec r-\Delta\vec r) &= \hat T^\dagger_{\Delta\vec r}\hat{\vec A}(\vec r)\hat T_{\Delta\vec r} \\ &= (\hat 1 + i\Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}/\hbar) \hat{\vec A}(\vec r) (\hat 1 - i\Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}/\hbar) \\ &= \hat{\vec A}(\vec r) + i\Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}/\hbar\hat{\vec A}(\vec r) - i\hat{\vec A}(\vec r)\Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}/\hbar + o((\Delta\vec r)^2) \\ &= \hat{\vec A}(\vec r) + \frac{i}{\hbar}[\Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}, \hat{\vec A}(\vec r)] \\ \frac{i}{\hbar}[\Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}, \hat{\vec A}(\vec r)] &= \hat{\vec A}(\vec r-\Delta\vec r) - \hat{\vec A}(\vec r) \end{align} Using the definition for the vector potential as defined in (11), we can find the left hand side: \begin{align} [\Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}, \hat{\vec A}(\vec r)] &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{hc^2}{V\omega} \left\{ \Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}\hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i}e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} + \Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i}e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r} - \hat a_{\vec k, i}\Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}\hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i}e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i} \Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i}e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r} \right\} \end{align} we can also find the right hand side also using (11), in the specific case where \(\Delta\vec r\) is very small: \begin{align} \hat{\vec A}(\vec r-\Delta\vec r) - \hat{\vec A}(\vec r) &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega}( \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i}e^{i\vec k\cdot(\vec r - \Delta\vec r)} + \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat\epsilon^*_{\vec k, i}e^{-i\vec k\cdot(\vec r - \Delta\vec r)} - \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i}e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\epsilon^*_{\vec k, i}e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r} ) \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega}( \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i}e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r}e^{-i\vec k\cdot \Delta\vec r} + \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat\epsilon^*_{\vec k, i}e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r}e^{i\vec k\cdot\Delta\vec r} - \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i}e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\epsilon^*_{\vec k, i}e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r} ) \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega}( \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i}e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r}(e^{-i\vec k\cdot \Delta\vec r} - 1) + \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat\epsilon^*_{\vec k, i}e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r}(e^{i\vec k\cdot\Delta\vec r} - 1) ) \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega}( \hat a_{\vec k, i} \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} (1 - i\vec k\Delta\vec r - 1) + \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i} \hat\epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r} (1 + i\vec k \cdot\Delta\vec r - 1) ) \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega}( \hat a_{\vec k, i} \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i} \hat\epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r} ) (-i\vec k\cdot\Delta\vec r) \\ \end{align} We now set the left hand side and the right hand side equal: \begin{align} \frac{i}{\hbar}[\Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}, \hat{\vec A}(\vec r)] &= \hat{\vec A}(\vec r-\Delta\vec r) - \hat{\vec A}(\vec r) \\ \frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{hc^2}{V\omega} \left\{ \Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}\hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i}e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} + \Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i}e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r} - \hat a_{\vec k, i}\Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}\hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i}e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i} \Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i}e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r} \right\} &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega}( \hat a_{\vec k, i} \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i} \hat\epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r} ) (-i\vec k\cdot\Delta\vec r) \\ \frac{1}{\hbar} \left\{ \Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}\hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i}e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} + \Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i}e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r} - \hat a_{\vec k, i}\Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}\hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i}e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i} \Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i}e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r} \right\} &= ( \hat a_{\vec k, i} \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i} \hat\epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r} ) (-\vec k\cdot\Delta\vec r) \\ \frac{1}{\hbar} \left\{ [\Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}, \hat a_{\vec k, i}]\hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i}e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} + [\Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}, \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}]\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i}e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r} \right\} &= ( \hat a_{\vec k, i} \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i} \hat\epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r} ) (-\vec k\Delta\cdot\vec r) \\ \end{align} Matching up term by term, we get: \begin{align} \frac{1}{\hbar}[\Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}, \hat a_{\vec k, i}]\hat\epsilon_{\vec k, i}e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} &= \hat a_{\vec k, i} \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} (-\vec k\cdot\Delta\vec r) \\ \frac{1}{\hbar}[\Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}, \hat a_{\vec k, i}] &= \hat a_{\vec k, i} (-\vec k\cdot\Delta\vec r) \\ [\Delta\vec r\cdot\hat{\vec p}, \hat a_{\vec k, i}] &= -\hbar \hat a_{\vec k, i} (\vec k\cdot\Delta\vec r) \\ \end{align} Since this is true for all \(\Delta\vec r\), \[ [\hat{\vec p}, \hat a_{\vec k, i}] = -\hbar\hat a_{\vec k, i}\vec k \] We can use this to find the linear momentum \(\vec p\) in terms of the creation and annihilation operators.
As an extension of what we already know from the quantum harmonic oscillators, we can say that: \[ [\hat a_{\vec k, i}, \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}] = \delta_{\vec k, \vec k'}\delta_{ij} \qquad \text{and} \qquad [\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}, \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k', j}] = [\hat a_{\vec k, i}, \hat a_{\vec k', j}] = 0 \] that is:
  • A creation operator and an annihilation operator commute if they correspond to different modes, but otherwise have a commutator of 1: this is because it DOES matter if you have already added/removed a photon to/from a specific mode, if you intend to add/remove a photon in that mode now.
  • Two creation operators or two annihilation operators always commute-- either they correspond to the same mode, in which case they are the same operator and commute, or they correspond to different modes, in which case they mind their own business and commute anyway.
If we take the commutator between some string of annilation and creation operators for a single mode that is \(n\) operators long, and the annihilation operator for that same mode, we end up with a string of \(n-1\) annihilation operators that. The cases that are the most of interest to us are: \begin{align} [\hat a, \hat a^\dagger] &= 1 \\ \hat a\hat a^\dagger - \hat a^\dagger\hat a &= 1 \\ \hat a\hat a^\dagger &= \hat a^\dagger\hat a + 1 \\ [\hat a^\dagger\hat a, \hat a] &= \hat a^\dagger\hat a\hat a - \hat a\hat a^\dagger\hat a \\ &= \hat a^\dagger\hat a\hat a - (\hat a^\dagger\hat a + 1)\hat a \\ &= \hat a^\dagger\hat a\hat a - \hat a^\dagger\hat a\hat a - \hat a \\ &= - \hat a \\ \end{align} Because \([\hat{\vec p}, \hat a_{\vec k, i}]\) contains a single \(-\hat a_{\vec k, i}\) for all modes, we know that \(\hat{\vec p}\) must contain a \(\hat a^\dagger\hat a\) for each mode: \[ \hat{\vec p} = \sum_{\vec k, i}\hbar\vec k \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat a_{\vec k, i} = \sum_{\vec k, i}\hat n_{\vec k, i} \cdot \hbar\vec k \] essentially, the sum over all modes of the number of photons in each mode times the momentum of a single photon, \(\hbar\vec k\).

Finding angular momentum. The classical expression is \[ \vec J = \frac{1}{4\pi c}\int d^3 r \quad \vec r\times(\vec E_\perp \times \vec B) \] which we shuffle around using \((\vec a\times\vec b)_i = \varepsilon_{ijk}a_j b_k\): \begin{align} \vec r \times (\vec E_\perp\times \vec B) &= \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk}\vec r_j(\vec E_\perp \times \vec B)_k \text{ (summation over }i\text{ implied)} \\ &= \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk}r_j(\varepsilon_{klm}E_l B_m) \\ &= \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} r_j\varepsilon_{klm}E_l\varepsilon_{mno}\partial_n A_o \end{align} where the last line is a result of \(\vec B = \vec\nabla\times\vec A\), which means that \(B_m\) specifically must be \(\varepsilon_{mno}\partial_n A_o\). We can now collapse some of the \(\varepsilon\) using the fact that \(\varepsilon_{klm}\varepsilon_{mno} = \delta_{kn}\delta_{lo} - \delta_{ko}\delta_{ln}\): \begin{align} \vec r \times (\vec E_\perp\times \vec B) &= \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} r_jE_l\varepsilon_{klm}\varepsilon_{mno}\partial_n A_o \\ &= \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} r_jE_l \partial_n A_o (\delta_{kn}\delta_{lo} - \delta_{ko}\delta_{ln}) \\ &= \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} r_jE_l \partial_n A_o \delta_{kn}\delta_{lo} - \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} r_jE_l \partial_n A_o \delta_{ko}\delta_{ln} \\ &= \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} r_jE_l \partial_k A_o \delta_{lo} - \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} r_jE_l \partial_n A_k \delta_{ln} \\ &= \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} r_jE_l \partial_k A_l - \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} r_j\textcolor{red}{E_l \partial_l} A_k \\ &= E_l[\hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} r_j \partial_k] A_l - \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} r_j\textcolor{red}{(\vec E_\perp\cdot\vec\nabla)} A_k\\ &= E_l(\vec r \times \vec\nabla) A_l - \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} r_j(\vec E_\perp\cdot\vec\nabla) A_k \end{align} This means that classical angular momentum is \begin{align} \vec J &= \frac{1}{4\pi c}\int d^3 r \quad \textcolor{red}{(E_l(\vec r \times \vec\nabla) A_l - \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} r_j(\vec E_\perp\cdot\vec\nabla) A_k)} \\ &= \frac{1}{4\pi c}\int d^3 r \quad E_l(\vec r \times \vec\nabla) A_l - \frac{1}{4\pi c}\int d^3 r \quad \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} r_j(\vec E_\perp\cdot\vec\nabla) A_k \\ \end{align} The first term, \(\frac{1}{4\pi c}\int d^3 r \quad E_l(\vec r \times \vec\nabla) A_l \), is the orbital angular momentum, which makes sense as it depends on \(\vec r\), or the distance from the origin. The second term can be manipulated as such: \begin{align} \vec\nabla\cdot(\vec E_\perp A_k) &= \vec\nabla\cdot\vec E_\perp A_k + \vec E_\perp\cdot\vec\nabla A_k \\ &= 0 + (\vec E_\perp\cdot\vec\nabla) A_k \\ -\frac{1}{4\pi c}\int d^3 r \quad \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} r_j(\vec E_\perp\cdot\vec\nabla) A_k &= -\frac{1}{4\pi c}\int d^3 r \quad \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} r_j\vec\nabla\cdot(\vec E_\perp A_k) \\ &= -\frac{1}{4\pi c}\int d^3 r \quad \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} r_j\vec\nabla\cdot(\vec E_\perp A_k) \\ \vec\nabla\cdot(r_j\vec E A_k) &= (\vec\nabla r_j)\vec E A_k + r_j\vec\nabla\cdot(\vec E A_k) \\ r_j\vec\nabla\cdot(\vec E A_k) &= \vec\nabla\cdot(r_j\vec E A_k) - (\vec\nabla r_j)\vec E A_k \\ -\frac{1}{4\pi c}\int d^3 r \quad \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} r_j(\vec E_\perp\cdot\vec\nabla) A_k &= -\frac{1}{4\pi c}\int d^3 r \quad \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} \left(\vec\nabla\cdot(r_j\vec E_\perp A_k) - (\vec\nabla r_j)\vec E_\perp A_k \right) \\ \end{align} We can expand \(\vec\nabla r_j\) into: \[ \vec\nabla r_j = \sum_i \hat u_i \frac{\partial r_j}{\partial r_i} = \sum_i \hat u_i\delta_{ij} = \hat u_j \text{ and } \hat u_j \vec E_\perp = E_j \] so we get: \begin{align} -\frac{1}{4\pi c}\int d^3 r \quad \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} \left(\vec\nabla\cdot(r_j\vec E A_k) - (\vec\nabla r_j)\vec E A_k \right) &= -\frac{1}{4\pi c}\int d^3 r \quad \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} \left(\vec\nabla\cdot(r_j\vec E A_k) - \vec E_j A_k \right) \end{align} If the fields vanish at large distances, then, using Gauss's theorem (\(\int_V d^3 r \vec\nabla \cdot \vec F = \int_{\partial V} d\vec a \cdot \vec F\)), we see: \[ \int_V d^3 r\vec\nabla\cdot(r_j\vec E_\perp A_k) = \int_{\partial V} d\vec a \cdot (r_j\vec E_\perp A_k) = 0 \] Thus our second term in angular momentum \(\vec J\) is: \[ -\frac{1}{4\pi c}\int d^3 r \quad \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} \left(0 - \vec E_j A_k \right) = \frac{1}{4\pi c}\int d^3 r \quad \hat u_i\varepsilon_{ijk} \vec E_j A_k = \frac{1}{4\pi c}\int d^3 r \vec E_\perp \times \vec A \] notice that this does not depend on the location of the origin, or \(\vec r\), and so it must be angular momentum that is intrinsic to the particle itself... like spin. We call this \(\vec J_s\). Now let's find \(\hat{\vec J_s}\), using (11) and (12): \begin{align} \frac{1}{4\pi c}\int d^3 r \vec E_\perp \times \vec A &\mapsto \frac{1}{4\pi c}\int d^3 r \hat{\vec E_\perp} \times \hat{\vec A} \\ &= \frac{1}{4\pi c}\int d^3 r \left(\sum_{\vec k, i} i\sqrt{\frac{h\omega}{V}}\left(\hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k \cdot\vec r}\right)\right) \times \left(\sum_{\vec k', j} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega'} \left( \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k', j} \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k', j} e^{-i\vec k' \cdot\vec r} + \hat a_{\vec k', j} \hat \epsilon_{\vec k', j} e^{i\vec k' \cdot\vec r}\right)\right) \\ &= \frac{ihc}{V4\pi c} \sum_{\vec k, \vec k', i, j} \sqrt\frac{\omega}{\omega'} \int d^3 r \Bigg\{ \left( \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \right) \times \left( \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k', j} \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k', j} e^{-i\vec k' \cdot\vec r} \right) + \left( \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \right) \times \left( \hat a_{\vec k', j} \hat \epsilon_{\vec k', j} e^{i\vec k' \cdot\vec r} \right) \\ &\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad - \left( \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \right) \times \left( \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k', j} \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k', j} e^{-i\vec k' \cdot\vec r} \right) - \left( \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \right) \times \left( \hat a_{\vec k', j} \hat \epsilon_{\vec k', j} e^{i\vec k' \cdot\vec r} \right) \Bigg\} \\ &= \frac{ih}{V4\pi} \sum_{\vec k, \vec k', i, j} \sqrt\frac{\omega}{\omega'} \int d^3 r \Bigg\{ \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k', j} \left( \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k', j} \right) e^{i(\vec k-\vec k') \cdot\vec r} + \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat a_{\vec k', j} \left( \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon_{\vec k', j} \right) e^{i(\vec k+\vec k') \cdot\vec r} \\ &\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k', j} \left( \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k', j} \right) e^{-i(\vec k - \vec k') \cdot\vec r} - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat a_{\vec k', j} \left( \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon_{\vec k', j} \right) e^{i(-\vec k + \vec k') \cdot\vec r} \Bigg\} \\ &= \frac{ih}{V4\pi} \sum_{\vec k, \vec k', i, j} \sqrt\frac{\omega}{\omega'} \Bigg\{ \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k', j} \left( \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k', j} \right) \int d^3 re^{i(\vec k-\vec k') \cdot\vec r} + \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat a_{\vec k', j} \left( \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon_{\vec k', j} \right) \int d^3 re^{i(\vec k+\vec k') \cdot\vec r} \\ &\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k', j} \left( \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k', j} \right) \int d^3 re^{-i(\vec k - \vec k') \cdot\vec r} - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat a_{\vec k', j} \left( \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon_{\vec k', j} \right) \int d^3 re^{i(-\vec k + \vec k') \cdot\vec r} \Bigg\} \end{align} Recall that to obtain (6), we used \[ \int_V d^3r\, e^{i(\vec k-\vec k')\cdot \vec r} = V\,\delta_{\vec k\vec k'} \] which also means that \( \int_V d^3r\, e^{i(\vec k+\vec k')\cdot \vec r} = V\,\delta_{\vec k, -\vec k'} \) and \( \int_V d^3r\, e^{-i(\vec k-\vec k')\cdot \vec r} = V\,\delta_{-\vec k, \vec k'} \) . We use it again here: \begin{align} \hat {\vec J}_s &= \frac{ih}{4\pi} \sum_{\vec k, i, j} \sqrt\frac{\omega}{\omega} \Bigg\{ \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, j} \left( \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, j} \right) + \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat a_{-\vec k, j} \left( \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon_{-\vec k, j} \right) - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat a^\dagger_{-\vec k, j} \left( \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{-\vec k, j} \right) - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat a_{\vec k, j} \left( \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, j} \right) \Bigg\} \\ &= \frac{ih}{4\pi} \sum_{\vec k, i, j} \Bigg\{ \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, j} \left( \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, j} \right) + \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat a_{-\vec k, j} \left( \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, j} \right) - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat a^\dagger_{-\vec k, j} \left( \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, j} \right) - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat a_{\vec k, j} \left( \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, j} \right) \Bigg\} \end{align} The \(\hat a \hat a\) and \(\hat a^\dagger \hat a^\dagger\) terms turn out to be 0. For example: \begin{align} \sum_{\vec k, i, j} \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat a_{-\vec k, j} \left( \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, j} \right) &= \sum_{\vec k, i, j} \hat a_{-\vec k, i}\hat a_{\vec k, j} \left( \hat \epsilon_{-\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{-\vec k, j} \right) \qquad \text{ (swap } \vec k \text{ and } \vec k' \text{ )} \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i, j} \hat a_{-\vec k, i}\hat a_{\vec k, j} \left( \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, j} \right) \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i, j} \hat a_{-\vec k, j}\hat a_{\vec k, i} \left( \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, j} \times \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \right) \qquad \text{(swap i and j)} \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i, j} \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat a_{-\vec k, j} \left( \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, j} \times \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \right) \qquad \text{(annihilation operators for different modes commute)} \\ &= -\sum_{\vec k, i, j} \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat a_{-\vec k, j} \left( \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, j} \right) \qquad \text{(cross product is anti-symmetric)} \\ \end{align} If a quantity \(S\) equals \(-S\), then \(S=0\). Thus \(\hat{\vec J}_s\) is: \[ \hat{\vec J}_s = \frac{ih}{4\pi} \sum_{\vec k, i, j} \Bigg\{ \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, j} \left( \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, j} \right) - \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat a_{\vec k, j} \left( \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, j} \right) \Bigg\} \] Or, if we swap \(i\) and \(j\) and use the anti-symmetry of the cross product in the second term only: \[ \hat{\vec J}_s = \frac{ih}{4\pi} \sum_{\vec k, i, j} \Bigg\{ \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, j} \left( \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, j} \right) + \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, j}\hat a_{\vec k, i} \left( \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, j} \right) \Bigg\} = \frac{ih}{4\pi} \sum_{\vec k, i, j} \Bigg\{ \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, j} + \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, j}\hat a_{\vec k, i} \Bigg\} \left( \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, j} \right) \]

Circular Polarization

It is convenient to express this result for a specific type of polarization. Say that the two unit vectors orthogonal to \(\vec k\) are \(\hat u_{\vec k, 1}\) and \(\hat u_{\vec k, 2}\). We define the following: \[ \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, \pm} \equiv \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}(\hat u_{\vec k, 1} \pm i\hat u_{\vec k, 2}) \] this is called a helicity basis. It describes a polarization vector with two components, one along \(\hat u_{\vec k, 1}\) and one along \(\hat u_{\vec k, 2}\), which are equal in magnitude.

If we, for example, plug this into an arbitrary field \(\vec E = E_0\hat \epsilon_\pm e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t)}\), we still need to find the real component (since the electric field is real and observable). Doing this, we get: \begin{align} \vec E_{actual} &= \text{Re}(E_0 \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}(\hat u_{\vec k, 1} \pm i\hat u_{\vec k, 2}) e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t)} ) \\ &= \frac{E_0}{\sqrt 2} \text{Re}(\hat u_{\vec k, 1}e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t)} \pm i\hat u_{\vec k, 2}e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t)} ) \\ &= \frac{E_0}{\sqrt 2} \text{Re}(\hat u_{\vec k, 1}(\cos(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t) + i\sin(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t)) \pm i\hat u_{\vec k, 2}(\cos(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t) + i\sin(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t))) \\ &= \frac{E_0}{\sqrt 2} (\hat u_{\vec k, 1}\cos(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t) \mp \hat u_{\vec k, 2}\sin(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t)) \\ \end{align} which clearly results in sinusoildal oscillation of the polarization being purely in \(\hat u_{\vec k, 1}\) when \(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t = n\pi, n \in \mathbb Z\), and purely in \(\hat u_{\vec k, 2}\) when it is equal to \(\pi/2, 3\pi/2,\) etc.

which describe the direction and magnitude of the electric field for polarization along \(\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, +} \) and \(\hat \epsilon_{\vec k, -} \) respectively.



We further define the following properties: \begin{align} \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \pm} &= \hat\epsilon_{\vec k, \mp} \\ \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, \pm} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \pm} &= \mp i\hat k \\ \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, \pm} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \mp} &= 0 \end{align} In this case, the angular momentum operator becomes: \begin{align} \hat{\vec J}_s &= \frac{ih}{4\pi} \sum_{\vec k, i, j} \Bigg\{ \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, j} + \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, j}\hat a_{\vec k, i} \Bigg\} \left( \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, j} \right) \\ &= \frac{ih}{4\pi} \sum_{\vec k, i} \Bigg\{ \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i} + \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat a_{\vec k, i} \Bigg\} \left( \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} \right) \qquad \text{(Because } \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, \pm} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \mp} = 0 \text{)}\\ &= \frac{ih}{4\pi} \sum_{\vec k, i} \Bigg\{ \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i} + \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat a_{\vec k, i} \Bigg\} \left( \mp i\hat k \right) \qquad \text{(Because } \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, \pm} \times \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \pm} = \mp i\hat k \text{)} \end{align} In this case, \(i\) can only be \(+\) or \(-\), and we have a factor of \(-i\hat k\) if it is \(+\) and a factor of \(+i\hat k\) if it is \(i\). Thus if we expand over \(i\), we get: \begin{align} \hat{\vec J}_s &= \frac{ih}{4\pi} \sum_{\vec k, i} \Bigg\{ \hat a_{\vec k, i}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i} + \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}\hat a_{\vec k, i} \Bigg\} \left( \mp i\hat k \right) \\ &= \frac{ih}{4\pi} \sum_{\vec k} \Bigg\{ \hat a_{\vec k, +}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, +} + \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, +}\hat a_{\vec k, +} \Bigg\} \left( - i\hat k \right) + \Bigg\{ \hat a_{\vec k, -}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, -} + \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, -}\hat a_{\vec k, -} \Bigg\} \left( i\hat k \right) \\ \end{align} Recall from our overview of the quantum harmonic oscillator last semester and our previous discussion on linear momentum that within one single mode, \([\hat a, \hat a^\dagger] = \hat a \hat a^\dagger - \hat a^\dagger\hat a = 1\). This means that \(\hat a \hat a^\dagger = \hat a^\dagger\hat a + 1\): \begin{align} \hat{\vec J}_s &= \frac{ih}{4\pi} \sum_{\vec k} \Bigg\{ 2\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, +}\hat a_{\vec k, +} + 1 \Bigg\} \left( - i\hat k \right) + \Bigg\{ 2\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, -}\hat a_{\vec k, -} + 1 \Bigg\} \left( i\hat k \right) \\ &= \frac{ih}{4\pi} \sum_{\vec k, \mu} 2\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, \mu}\hat a_{\vec k, \mu} (-i\mu\hat k) \\ &= \frac{i\hbar}{2} \sum_{\vec k, \mu} 2\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, \mu}\hat a_{\vec k, \mu} (-i\mu\hat k) \text{ (Using } h = 2\pi\hbar \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, \mu} \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, \mu}\hat a_{\vec k, \mu} (\hbar\mu\hat k) = \sum_{\vec k, \mu} \hbar\hat k \mu \hat n_{\vec k, \mu} \\ &= \sum_{\vec k} \hbar\hat k \hat n_{\vec k, +} - \hat k\hbar \hat n_{\vec k, -} = \sum_{\vec k} \hbar\hat k (\hat n_{\vec k, +} - \hat n_{\vec k, -}) \end{align} where we re-introduce polarization label \(i\) as \(\mu\) to avoid confusion with the imaginary \(i\).

We saw earlier that the linear momentum of a single photon is \(\hbar \vec k\), which has a magnitude of \(\hbar |\vec k|\). We see now that the intrinsic angular momentum of a single photon is \(\hbar \hat k\), which has a magnitude of \(\hbar\). Because the intrinsic angular momentum strictly has a magnitude of \(\hbar\), we say that photons are spin-1 particles.

Note that there is no \(\mu=0\), or a longitudinally polarized photon, which is fundamentally different from massive particles. Since photons are massless and move at the speed of light, \(c\), there is no frame in which they would be at rest... in a way, this demonstration also shows that photons are massless. I guess.

Coherent States

We can see that the expectation values of \(\hat {\vec A}, \hat {\vec E}, \hat {\vec B}\) are all 0 if there is a defined number of photons in the field, even if they have non-zero total energy \(n\hbar\omega\) and non-zero total momentum \(n\hbar\vec k\). This is beacuse all three of these operators have both creation and annihilation terms, each for a specific mode. Thus the expectation value has terms that look like the following: \begin{align} \langle n_{\vec k, i} | \hat a_{\vec k, i} | n_{\vec k, i}\rangle \text{ and } \langle n_{\vec k, i} | \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i} | n_{\vec k, i}\rangle \end{align} for every single mode. These, of course, all result in 0's: \begin{align} \langle n_{\vec k, i} | \hat a_{\vec k, i} | n_{\vec k, i}\rangle &= \sqrt n \langle n_{\vec k, i} | (n-1)_{\vec k, i}\rangle \\ &= 0 \\ \langle n_{\vec k, i} | \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i} | n_{\vec k, i}\rangle &= \sqrt{n + 1} \langle n_{\vec k, i} | (n+1)_{\vec k, i}\rangle \\ &= 0 \end{align} since the basis vectors in each mode are orthogonal to each other. Clearly, fields that have an expectation value of 0 even with definite total energy and momentum, are non-classical. We now have the possibility of entangled states as well; recall that we can define a total state as follows: \[ |\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2!}}(\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i})^2 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{5!}}(\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k', j})^5 \ket - \] see above where the state contains two photons in mode \(\vec k, i\) AND five photons in mode \(\vec k', j\). We can just as well define a state like this: \[ |\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2!}}(\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i})^2 + \frac{1}{\sqrt{5!}}(\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k', j})^5 \ket - \] where the state either contains two photons in mode \(\vec k, i\), OR five photons in mode \(\vec k', j\).

There is, however, a class of states that DO correspond to classical fields. One such type of state is the coherent state, which are eigenstates of the annihilation operator: \begin{align} \hat a |\alpha\rangle = \alpha|\alpha\rangle, &\quad |\alpha\rangle = e^{-\alpha^2/2}\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}} |n\rangle, \quad \alpha\in\mathbb C \\ \langle \alpha | \hat a | \alpha\rangle = \alpha, &\quad \langle \alpha | \hat a^\dagger | \alpha\rangle = \alpha^*, \quad \langle \alpha_{\vec k, i} | \hat a_{\vec k', j} | \alpha_{\vec k, i} \rangle = \delta_{\vec k, \vec k'}\delta_{ij}\alpha_{\vec k, i} \end{align} note that these are not obervable states, as these are complex. Over time, the eigenvalues for these states rotate clockwise in the complex plane: \[ |\psi(t=0)\rangle = |\alpha\rangle, \quad |\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-i\hat H t/\hbar}|\alpha\rangle = |\alpha e^{-i\omega t}\rangle = |\alpha(t)\rangle \] and they do not always have an expectation value of 0: \begin{align} \langle \hat{\vec A}(\vec r)\rangle_{\alpha(t)} &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} \langle\alpha(t)|\left[ \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i} \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k \cdot\vec r} + \hat a_{\vec k, i} \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \right]|\alpha(t)\rangle \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} \langle\alpha e^{-i\omega t}|\left[ \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i} \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k \cdot\vec r} + \hat a_{\vec k, i} \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \right]|\alpha e^{-i\omega t}\rangle \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} \left[ \langle\alpha e^{-i\omega t}|\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, i}|\alpha e^{-i\omega t}\rangle \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k \cdot\vec r} + \langle\alpha e^{-i\omega t}|\hat a_{\vec k, i}|\alpha e^{-i\omega t}\rangle \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \right] \\ &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} \left[ \alpha^* e^{i\omega t} \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k \cdot\vec r} + \alpha e^{-i\omega t} \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \right] \\ \end{align} Let us arbitrarily say that \(\vec k\) is aligned with the z-axis, and say that \(\hat z\cdot\vec r = \vec r_z = z\): \begin{align} \langle \hat{\vec A}(\vec r)\rangle_{\alpha(t)} &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} \left[ \alpha^* e^{i\omega t} \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, i} e^{-i\vec k \cdot\vec r} + \alpha e^{-i\omega t} \hat \epsilon_{\vec k, i} e^{i\vec k \cdot\vec r} \right] \\ &= \sum_{i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} \left[ \alpha^* e^{i\omega t} \hat \epsilon^*_{k\cdot\hat z, i} e^{-ik\cdot\hat z \cdot\vec r} + \alpha e^{-i\omega t} \hat \epsilon_{k\cdot\hat z, i} e^{ik\cdot\hat z \cdot\vec r} \right] \\ &= \sum_{i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} \left[ \alpha^* e^{i\omega t} \hat \epsilon^*_{k\cdot\hat z, i} e^{-ikz} + \alpha e^{-i\omega t} \hat \epsilon_{k\cdot\hat z, i} e^{ikz} \right] \\ \end{align} we can thus say that the two options for \(\epsilon_{k\cdot\hat z, i}\) are \(\hat x\) and \(\hat y\), since those form an orthonormal basis with our \(\hat k\). Let's say that we're polarized along \(\hat x\) (though the result is identical for \(\hat y\)). It's complex conjugate is just \(\hat x\); thus we get: \begin{align} \langle \hat{\vec A}(\vec r)\rangle_{\alpha(t)} &= \sum_{i} \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} \left[ \alpha^* e^{i\omega t} \hat \epsilon^*_{k\cdot\hat z, i} e^{-ikz} + \alpha e^{-i\omega t} \hat \epsilon_{k\cdot\hat z, i} e^{ikz} \right] \\ &= \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} \hat x \left[ \alpha^* e^{i\omega t} e^{-ikz} + \alpha e^{-i\omega t} e^{ikz} \right] \\ &= \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} \hat x \left[ |\alpha| e^{-i\phi} e^{i\omega t} e^{-ikz} + |\alpha| e^{i\phi} e^{-i\omega t} e^{ikz} \right] \quad \text{Since } \alpha \in \mathbb C, \alpha = |\alpha| e^{i\phi} \\ &= \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} \hat x \left[ |\alpha| e^{-i\phi + i\omega t - ikz} + |\alpha| e^{i\phi - i\omega t + ikz} \right] \\ &= \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} \hat x \left[ |\alpha| e^{-i(\phi - \omega t + kz)} + |\alpha| e^{i(\phi - \omega t + kz)} \right] \\ &= \sqrt\frac{h c^2}{V\omega} 2 \hat x |\alpha| \cos(\phi - \omega t + kz) \\ &= A_0 \hat x \cos(\phi - \omega t + kz) \end{align} which is clearly not 0 for all \(t\), and thus replicates a classical state. This is similar for both the electric and magnetic fields. In fact, one can reproduce any classical state for all three fields by combining coherent states.

The Total Hamiltonian for Radiation and Matter

So far we have only looked at the system that contains only the electromagnetic field, or the radiation. What if we also consider a system of charged particles which interact with the field? The total Hamiltonian would then be: \[ \hat H = \hat H_{rad} + \hat H_{matter} + \hat H' \] where \(\hat H'\) is some time-independent interaction related to the kinetic energy of each charge particle \(i\): \[ \hat H' = -\sum_i \frac{q_i}{m_i c} \cdot \hat{\vec A}(\hat{\vec r}_i)\cdot\hat{\vec p_i} + \sum_i\frac{q_i^2}{2m_i c^2} \hat{\vec A}^2(\hat{\vec r}_i) \] Under the dipole approximation, we set \(\vec r_i\) to 0 and if the wavelength of all modes are much larger than the size of the matter system, the spatial dependence of the field can be neglected, so the second term becomes a global constant that can be often removed (with a unitary transformation): \[ \hat H' \approx -\sum_i \frac{q_i}{m_i c} \cdot \hat{\vec A}(\vec 0)\cdot\hat{\vec p_i} \] Since this interaction/perturbation is time-independent, we can apply the TDPT generalization where the perturbation does not change with time: \[ \mathcal A_{f\leftarrow i}^{(n)} = \frac{2\pi}{i\hbar}\langle f | \hat O \left[\hat G_0^+(E_i)\hat O\right]^{n-1} | i\rangle \delta_T(\omega_{fi}) \] thus the energy of the inital and final state must be the same (39:00). For example, let's say that our initial state is: \[ |i\rangle = |1s\rangle \otimes \frac{(\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, \lambda})^n}{\sqrt{n!}} |-\rangle = |1s\rangle \otimes |n_{\vec k, \lambda}\rangle \] and our final state is: \[ |f\rangle = |2p\rangle \otimes |(n-1)_{\vec k, \lambda}\rangle \] so we are working with electrons, where \(q_i = -e\) and \(m_i = m_e\). The first-order transition amplitude is thus: \begin{align} \mathcal A^{(1)}_{f\leftarrow i} &= \frac{2\pi}{i\hbar}\delta_T(0) \langle f | \hat H' | i\rangle \\ &= \frac{e}{m_e c}\frac{2\pi}{i\hbar} \langle 2p | \langle n-1 | \hat{\vec A}(\vec 0) |n\rangle \cdot \hat{\vec p}_i|1s\rangle \\ \end{align} only the destructor term, \(\sqrt\frac{hc^2}{V\omega}\hat a_{\vec k, \lambda}\hat\epsilon_{\vec k, \lambda}e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec 0}\), in the vector potential operator does anything, so: \[ \langle n-1 | \hat{\vec A}(\vec 0) |n\rangle = \langle n-1 | \sqrt\frac{hc^2}{V\omega}\hat a_{\vec k, \lambda}\hat\epsilon_{\vec k, \lambda}e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec 0} |n\rangle = \sqrt\frac{hc^2}{V\omega} \hat\epsilon_{\vec k, \lambda} \sqrt n \] which means that the entire transition amplitude is: \[ \frac{e}{m_e c}\frac{2\pi}{i\hbar}\sqrt\frac{hnc^2}{V\omega} \langle 2p|\hat{\vec p}_i\cdot\hat\epsilon_{\vec k, \lambda} | 1s\rangle \]

Spontaneous Emission

In perturbation theory previously, only stimulated emission allowed a system to emit photons, implying that in the absence of radiation, the state would be infinitely stable. For example, \(\ket i = \ket e \otimes \ket -\) would never decay.

However, now that we have quantized the electric field, we see spontaneous emission/decay: \[ A^*_{(b)} \to A_{(a)} + \gamma_{\vec k, \lambda} \] Say we define the initial and final states of the system as \(\ket i = \ket e \otimes \ket -\) and \(\ket f = \ket g \otimes \ket 1_{\vec k, \lambda}\), for an excited state \(\ket e\) and a ground state \(\ket g\). There is no interaction between the system and an external field that causes this decay, hence we don't use the interaction Hamiltonian \(\hat H'\) to calculate the transition amplitude, like we did previously.

Instead, we say that simply the quantized field of the vacuum, \(\hat{\vec {\mathcal E}}\), causes the decay, and just as in a typical TDPT problem, we use \(-\hat{\vec {\mathcal E}} \cdot \hat{\vec\mu}\) as the perturbation. We can now calculate the transition amplitude: \begin{align} \mathcal A^{(1)}_{fi} &= \frac{2\pi}{i\hbar}\langle f | -\hat{\vec {\mathcal E}} \cdot \hat{\vec\mu} | i\rangle \delta_T(\omega_{fi}) \\ &= -\frac{2\pi}{i\hbar}\langle g | \langle 1 | \hat{\vec {\mathcal E}} |-\rangle \cdot \hat{\vec\mu} | e \rangle \delta_T(\omega_{fi}) \\ &= -\frac{2\pi}{i\hbar}\langle g | \langle 1 | -i\sqrt\frac{h\omega}{V}\hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, \lambda} \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \lambda} e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec 0} |-\rangle \cdot \hat{\vec\mu} | e \rangle \delta_T(\omega_{fi}) \\ &= i\sqrt\frac{h\omega}{V}\frac{2\pi}{i\hbar}\langle g | \langle 1 | \hat a^\dagger_{\vec k, \lambda} |-\rangle \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \lambda} \cdot \hat{\vec\mu} | e \rangle \delta_T(\omega_{fi}) \\ &= i\sqrt\frac{h\omega}{V}\frac{2\pi}{i\hbar}\langle g | \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \lambda} \cdot \hat{\vec\mu} | e \rangle \delta_T(\omega_{fi}) \\ &= i\sqrt\frac{h\omega}{V}\frac{2\pi}{i\hbar}\vec \mu_{ge} \cdot \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \lambda} \delta_T(\omega_{fi}) \\ \end{align} The initial energy of the system is the energy of the excited state, \(E_e\). The final energy of the system is teh energy of the ground state plus the energy of the emitted photon, \(E_g + \hbar\omega_{\vec k}\). This means that \(\omega_{fi} = (E_g + \hbar\omega_{\vec k} - E_e)/\hbar\), which we call \(\omega_{\vec k} - \Delta E/\hbar\). So we have: \begin{align} \mathcal A^{(1)}_{fi} &= \sqrt\frac{h\omega}{V}\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\vec \mu_{ge} \cdot \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \lambda} \delta_T(\omega_{\vec k} - \Delta E/\hbar) \\ \end{align} This is, of course, the transition amplitude from intial to final state where the final state's emitted photon has a very specific, single mode. We can, in reality, transition from the initial to final state and have the emitted photon be in any mode. The single transition rate can be calculated as follows: \begin{align} W^{(1)}_{\vec k, \lambda\leftarrow i} &= \left(\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\right)^2\frac{h\omega}{V} |\vec \mu_{ge} \cdot \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \lambda}|^2\delta(\omega_{\vec k} - \Delta E/\hbar) \frac{T}{2\pi}\frac{1}{T} \\ &= \frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\right)^2\frac{2\pi\hbar\omega}{V}|\vec \mu_{ge} \cdot \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \lambda}|^2\delta(\omega_{\vec k} - \Delta E/\hbar) \\ &= \frac{(2\pi)^2}{\hbar}\frac{\omega}{V}|\vec \mu_{ge} \cdot \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \lambda}|^2\delta(\omega_{\vec k} - \Delta E/\hbar) \end{align} From the single transition rate, we can find the total transition rate by doing: \[ \sum_{\vec k, i} W^{(1)}_{\vec k, \lambda\leftarrow i} = \sum_{\vec k, i} \frac{(2\pi)^2}{\hbar}\frac{\omega}{V}|\vec \mu_{ge} \cdot \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \lambda}|^2\delta(\omega_{\vec k} - \Delta E/\hbar) \] and use the identity that \(\sum_{\vec k} = \frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}\int d^3k\).

Remember that \(V = L^3\) is the volume of our box with periodic boundary conditions that we used to discretize momentum space. We have also constrained \(\vec k\) to be: \[ \vec k = \frac{2\pi}{L} n_x \hat x + \frac{2\pi}{L} n_y \hat y + \frac{2\pi}{L} n_z \hat z, \] Our \(\vec k\)'s are not continuous; each component of \(\vec k\) can only change by intervals of \(\frac{2\pi}{L}\). This means that an entire cubic area of \(\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}\right)^3\) corresponds to a single \(\vec k\).

We can estimate the number of valid \(\vec k\)'s in a given volume \(d^3 k\) by doing \[ \frac{d^3k}{(2\pi/L)^3} = \frac{L^3}{(2\pi)^3}d^3k = \frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}d^3k \]
A summation over all \(\vec k\), or \(\sum_{\vec k} 1\), counts the total number of discrete \(\vec k\)'s that exist. We can equivalently produce this count by computing \(\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}d^3k\).

If we make the volume of our box larger and larger and count the number of \(\vec k\)'s that exist in the entire volume, we add up the number of \(\vec k\)'s that exist in every tiny piece of volume in the total volume. Thus we do \(\int\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3} d^3k\). So we see that: \[ \sum_{\vec k} = \int\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3} d^3k = \frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}\int d^3k \]


So, we find the total transition rate by doing the following: \begin{align} \sum_{\vec k, i} W^{(1)}_{\vec k, \lambda\leftarrow i} &= \sum_{\vec k, i} \frac{(2\pi)^2}{\hbar}\frac{\omega}{V}|\vec \mu_{ge} \cdot \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \lambda}|^2\delta(\omega_{\vec k} - \Delta E/\hbar) \\ &= \sum_{i} \frac{V}{(2\pi)^3} \int d^3k \frac{(2\pi)^2}{\hbar}\frac{\omega}{V}|\vec \mu_{ge} \cdot \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \lambda}|^2\delta(\omega_{\vec k} - \Delta E/\hbar) \\ &= \frac{1}{2\pi\hbar} \sum_{i} \int d^3k \omega|\vec \mu_{ge} \cdot \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \lambda}|^2\delta(\omega_{\vec k} - \Delta E/\hbar) \\ &= \frac{1}{2\pi\hbar} \sum_{i} \int dk k^2 \int d\theta\sin\theta \int d\phi \omega|\vec \mu_{ge} \cdot \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \lambda}|^2\delta(\omega_{\vec k} - \Delta E/\hbar) \\ \text{Using } \omega = ck &\implies d\omega = c dk \implies dk = \frac{1}{c} d\omega \\ &= \frac{1}{2\pi\hbar} \sum_{i} \int \left(\frac{1}{c}d\omega\right) \left(\frac{\omega}{c}\right)^2 \int d\theta\sin\theta \int d\phi \omega|\vec \mu_{ge} \cdot \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \lambda}|^2\delta(\omega_{\vec k} - \Delta E/\hbar) \\ &= \frac{1}{2\pi\hbar c^3} \sum_{i} \int d\omega \int d\theta\sin\theta \int d\phi \omega^3 |\vec \mu_{ge} \cdot \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \lambda}|^2\delta(\omega_{\vec k} - \Delta E/\hbar) \end{align} At this point, the \(\delta(\omega_{\vec k} - \Delta E/\hbar)\) term collapses the integral over \(d\omega\) and sets \(\omega = \Delta E/\hbar\). \begin{align} \sum_{\vec k, i} W^{(1)}_{\vec k, \lambda\leftarrow i} &= \frac{1}{2\pi\hbar c^3} \sum_{i} \int d\omega \int d\theta\sin\theta \int d\phi \omega^3 |\vec \mu_{ge} \cdot \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \lambda}|^2\delta(\omega_{\vec k} - \Delta E/\hbar) \\ &= \frac{1}{2\pi\hbar c^3} \left(\frac{\Delta E}{\hbar}\right)^3 \sum_{i} \int d\theta\sin\theta \int d\phi |\vec \mu_{ge} \cdot \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \lambda}|^2 \\ &= \frac{1}{2\pi\hbar c^3} \left(\frac{\Delta E}{\hbar}\right)^3 \int d\theta\sin\theta \int d\phi \sum_{i} |\vec \mu_{ge} \cdot \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \lambda}|^2 \\ \end{align} We can write any vector \(\vec \mu\) as a sum of components along the \(\vec k\), \(\hat\epsilon^*_{\vec k, 1}\), and \(\hat\epsilon^*_{\vec k, 2}\), since all three of these are orthogonal. We can write the dot product between these vectors and \(\vec \mu\) as: \(\vec \mu\cdot\vec k = \vec\mu_k\), \(\vec \mu\cdot\hat\epsilon^*_{\vec k, 1} = \vec\mu_1\), and \(\vec \mu\cdot\hat\epsilon^*_{\vec k, 2} = \vec\mu_2\), given that \[ \vec\mu = \vec\mu_k\hat k + \vec\mu_1\hat\epsilon^*_{\vec k, 1} + \vec\mu_2\hat\epsilon^*_{\vec k, 2} \] we can also easily see that \[ |\vec\mu|^2 = |\vec\mu_k|^2 + |\vec\mu_1|^2 + |\vec\mu_2|^2 \] which means that if we want \(\sum_i\vec\mu\cdot\hat\epsilon^*_{\vec k, i}\), we can equivalently do \(|\vec \mu|^2 - |\vec\mu\cdot\vec k|^2\).

Furthermore, if we chose \(\vec k\) to align with the z-axis: \[ |\vec \mu|^2 - |\vec\mu\cdot\vec k|^2 = |\vec \mu|^2 - |\vec\mu|^2\cos^2\theta = |\vec\mu|^2\sin^2\theta \] since \(\theta\) is defined as the angle between \(\vec k\) (or whatever the vector of interest is) and the z-axis. Now our transition rate is: \begin{align} \sum_{\vec k, i} W^{(1)}_{\vec k, \lambda\leftarrow i} &= \frac{1}{2\pi\hbar c^3} \left(\frac{\Delta E}{\hbar}\right)^3 \int d\theta\sin\theta \int d\phi \sum_{i} |\vec \mu_{ge} \cdot \hat \epsilon^*_{\vec k, \lambda}|^2 \\ &= \frac{1}{2\pi\hbar c^3} \left(\frac{\Delta E}{\hbar}\right)^3 \int d\theta\sin\theta \int d\phi |\vec\mu_{ge}|^2\sin^2\theta \\ &= \frac{1}{\hbar c^3} \left(\frac{\Delta E}{\hbar}\right)^3 |\vec\mu_{ge}|^2 \int d\theta \sin^3\theta \\ &= \frac{1}{\hbar c^3} \left(\frac{\Delta E}{\hbar}\right)^3 |\vec\mu_{ge}|^2 \left[\frac{1}{3}\cos\theta-\cos\theta\right]_0^\pi \\ &= \frac{1}{\hbar c^3} \left(\frac{\Delta E}{\hbar}\right)^3 |\vec\mu_{ge}|^2 \left[-\frac{1}{3}+1 - \frac{1}{3} + 1\right] \\ &= \frac{4}{3\hbar c^3} \left(\frac{\Delta E}{\hbar}\right)^3 |\vec\mu_{ge}|^2\\ \end{align} or, if you prefer, \(\frac{4\omega^3}{3\hbar c^3}|\vec\mu_{ge}|^2\).
